Ant colonies produce strikingly different adult forms, queens and workers, from the same genome, which makes them a powerful system for studying how development generates morphological diversity. The *ergatoid* supergene adds a genetic switch to this picture. Queen larvae that carry it are diverted from the winged queen pathway and instead develop into a wingless form with a body intermediate between queens and workers. We found that the supergene acts on the processes that connect larval growth to the timing of metamorphosis, rather than by simply removing wings.

We are now working to identify the specific genes and molecular pathways within the supergene that drive this shift, and how they interact with the hormonal and nutritional signals that normally regulate caste development. Because the links between growth, body size, and the development of structures like wings are shared across insects, this work addresses a broad question in developmental biology: how can changes in a small number of genes reorganize an entire body plan? The answers help explain how novel forms arise during evolution.

The effects of *ergatoid* likely extend beyond morphology. Earlier studies suggest the supergene also influences worker behavior, reproduction, and colony organization, all of which could shape the success of the two queen types. A supergene might produce these linked traits through a single shared pathway or through many independent changes spread across its length, and we are testing which of these explanations applies to *ergatoid*.
